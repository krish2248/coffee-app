The brewer

Why the V60 is the benchmark

A cone, a paper filter, and a spiral of ribs. The Hario V60 looks simple, but every detail is a variable you can control — which is exactly why it became the reference brewer for specialty coffee.

01

60-degree cone

The steep walls create a deep coffee bed. Water travels through more grounds, extending contact time and pulling out clarity rather than muddiness.

02

Spiral ribs

Raised ribs hold the paper off the ceramic, letting air escape and water flow evenly. The result is a faster, more consistent drawdown.

03

Single large hole

Unlike flat-bed brewers with restricted flow, the V60 lets you steer extraction with grind size, pour speed, and pour structure.

04

Paper filter clarity

Rinsed paper traps fines and oils, producing the tea-like transparency that defines a great light-roast pour-over.

Roast levels

Roast is a design decision

Roasting develops sweetness and aroma while reducing acidity and origin character. In specialty coffee, lighter is generally the starting point — but the right roast depends on the bean, the brewer, and what you want in the cup.

Light Medium-light Medium Medium-dark Dark

Light

Internal temp ≈ 196–205°C · Agtron 70–80

Bright acidity, floral and fruit-forward, origin-transparent. Can taste grassy or underdeveloped if roasted too fast.

  • V60, Chemex, AeroPress
  • Higher water temp: 94–96°C

Medium-light

≈ 205–212°C · Agtron 60–70

The specialty sweet spot. Balanced acidity, caramel sweetness, and clear origin notes without roast dominance.

  • V60, Kalita, espresso
  • Water temp: 92–95°C

Medium

≈ 212–220°C · Agtron 50–60

Chocolate, nuts, and cooked fruit. Acidity is softened and body increases. A crowd-pleasing, versatile roast.

  • V60, drip, espresso
  • Water temp: 90–94°C

Medium-dark

≈ 220–230°C · Agtron 40–50

Dark chocolate, molasses, toasted nuts. Origin character starts to recede behind roast flavor and heavier body.

  • Espresso, milk drinks
  • Water temp: 88–92°C

Dark

≈ 230–245°C · Agtron 30–40

Smoky, bittersweet, low acidity. The roast — not the bean — leads. Common in traditional espresso and supermarket blends.

  • Espresso, French press
  • Water temp: 85–90°C

Temperatures are bean-temperature approximations; Agtron values vary by roaster and scale. Always ask your roaster for their reference points.

Flavor

A vocabulary for what you taste

Flavor is aroma, acidity, sweetness, bitterness, body, and finish working together. These six families are a practical shortcut to describing — and choosing — coffee.

Fruity berry · citrus · stone fruit

Natural-process coffees and Ethiopian heirlooms lean here. Look for blueberry, blackcurrant, peach, lemon, and dried fig.

BlueberryBlackcurrantPeachBergamotFig

Floral jasmine · rose · orange blossom

The hallmark of high-grown Geisha and washed Ethiopian coffees. Delicate, aromatic, and easily buried by dark roasting.

JasmineRoseHoneysuckleLavender

Sweet caramel · honey · brown sugar

The result of Maillard reactions during roasting. Medium roasts amplify it; under-developed coffee tastes sweet but hollow.

CaramelHoneyPanelaMaple

Nutty & cocoa almond · hazelnut · chocolate

Comforting and familiar. Common in Brazilian and Central American coffees, especially at medium and medium-dark roasts.

AlmondHazelnutCocoaDark chocolate

Spice cinnamon · clove · black pepper

Often a sign of complexity rather than heat. Spice notes pair well with milk and darker roast profiles.

CinnamonCloveCardamomPepper

Earthy & green herbal · woody · vegetal

Can be desirable savoriness (tomato leaf, cedar) or a defect (raw potato, mold). Context and intensity matter.

How to taste

Four steps, no snobbery required

  1. AromaSmell before you sip. Most of flavor is retronasal.
  2. SlurpAerate the coffee to spread it across your palate.
  3. StructureNotice acidity, sweetness, body, and balance.
  4. FinishWait ten seconds. The aftertaste tells you about quality.

The case

Why specialty is worth it

Specialty is not just a price point. It is a quality standard, a supply-chain relationship, and a way of brewing that rewards attention.

01

A measurable standard

Coffee scoring 80+ on the 100-point SCA cupping scale has documented sweetness, acidity, flavor, and cleanliness — not just marketing language.

02

Traceable origin

Farm, producer, altitude, variety, and process are usually on the bag. You can taste a place, and the farmer can be paid for it.

03

Freshness matters

Coffee is agricultural. Roast dates, harvest cycles, and storage affect the cup far more than most equipment upgrades.

04

Better economics

Direct-trade and transparent pricing models can pay producers well above commodity rates, supporting quality and resilience.

05

You control the cup

Grind, ratio, water, temperature, and pour structure are all yours. A modest setup can outperform a bad café.

06

Depth of experience

From tea-like Geisha to syrupy natural espresso, specialty covers a sensory range that commodity coffee rarely touches.

History

From legend to third wave

Coffee has been traded for centuries. Specialty coffee is recent: a quality movement that turned a commodity into a craft.

  1. c. 850

    The legend of Kaldi

    Ethiopian lore credits a goat herder with noticing the energizing effect of coffee cherries. The plant’s true home is the Ethiopian highlands.

  2. 1400s

    Yemen and the port of Mocha

    Sufi communities in Yemen brew and trade coffee. Mocha becomes synonymous with the drink and the port.

  3. 1600s

    Coffee houses spread across Europe

    London, Paris, and Vienna turn coffee into a social institution — and a political one.

  4. 1908

    Melitta Bentz invents the paper filter

    A German housewife patents a blotting-paper filter, making clean, sediment-free brewing a household possibility.

  5. 1974

    “Specialty coffee” gets a name

    Erna Knutsen coins the term to describe high-quality beans from specific micro-lots — the origin of the category.

  6. 1982

    The SCAA is founded

    The Specialty Coffee Association of America formalizes grading, education, and quality standards for the industry.

  7. 2002

    80 points becomes the line

    The industry converges on the 100-point cupping scale, with 80+ generally accepted as the specialty threshold.

  8. 2000s

    The third wave

    Roasters treat coffee like wine: single origins, light roasts, precise brewing, and provenance-forward menus.

  9. 2010s

    Home brewing goes pro

    V60, AeroPress, and digital scales move café techniques into home kitchens. World Brewers Cup popularizes recipes.

  10. 2024+

    A mainstream premium market

    Specialty coffee becomes a multi-billion-dollar global segment, with growth led by Asia-Pacific and direct-to-consumer subscriptions.
